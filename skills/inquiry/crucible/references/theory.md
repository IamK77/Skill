# Theory as a source of mechanisms and questions

## Use a formal shape to notice something

A conservation law, an invariant, a symmetry, a game, or a dynamical system can suggest a mechanism. State what is being borrowed and which assumptions are doing the work. An analogy is not a proof that the target system has the source system's properties.

## Try a minimal model

Reduce the situation to a few entities and one nontrivial interaction. Ask what the model excludes, then change an assumption. The resulting disagreement can be more informative than an elaborate derivation built on an unnoticed premise.

## Example

A heuristic appears to improve by retaining diverse candidates. A population model suggests competition and niches; an information view suggests coverage of uncertainty. These frames imply different measurements and failure cases. They can guide experiments without being presented as established explanations.

## When a formal claim is intended

Write assumptions, definitions, and the exact statement before supplying a derivation. Distinguish proof, conjecture, heuristic argument, and empirical support. A counterexample may narrow the theorem or reveal a useful new regime. Not every worthwhile research idea needs a theorem before a prototype can teach something.
