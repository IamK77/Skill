# A literature map that can generate questions

## Change the columns

A table organised by author and year supports recall. A table organised by mechanism, assumption, failure regime, resource cost, and observed population can reveal unexpected neighbours. Choose columns that serve the question rather than filling a universal matrix.

## Keep evidence and inference apart

Record where a claim appears, what the source actually establishes, and what you infer from comparing it with another source. Missing information should remain unknown. A method being popular does not establish that it fits the phenomenon of interest.

## Reproduction as exploration

Reproducing a small result can expose an assumption, a data convention, or a sensitivity hidden in prose. It is optional at the question-generation stage and should be labelled as a bounded experiment, with its actual environment and limitations recorded.

## Example

Two methods claim similar quality but measure different populations or budgets. Reorganising the table around those boundaries can turn an apparent competition into a question about transfer between regimes. A failed reproduction can be an environment mismatch, an incomplete report, or a substantive issue; investigate before choosing the explanation.

## Possible outputs

A useful result might be an annotated map, a pair of contradictory claims, a missing measurement, or a small reproducible observation. None requires an immediate project ranking or a committed experimental plan.
