# The museum of futures that never arrived

A museum displays no surviving objects. Every room is dedicated to something that almost happened: a train nobody boarded, a friendship interrupted by an unopened letter, an invention abandoned because its maker became interested in a garden.

The curator has trouble arranging the rooms chronologically. One future has remained possible for a hundred years. Another lasted only the second before somebody said yes. She starts ordering them by how far a visitor can still walk into them.

Stay with that peculiar measure for a while. Time is becoming something like depth rather than a row of dates. An old possibility may feel close; yesterday's may already be unreachable. A visitor might make a room shallower by understanding it, or open a door the curator thought was painted on the wall.

What would the museum record when a possibility becomes real? Perhaps the room disappears. Perhaps it stays, devoted to all the other ways the event might have happened. Perhaps the museum is relieved to lose a room and a little sorry at the same time.

There is no obligation to translate this into logical clocks. We might instead become interested in systems that remember commitments not yet made, communities organised around different senses of “too late,” or a history that changes because someone has found a new question to ask of it.

Those possibilities can remain uneven. A single door in the museum may be more interesting than a complete theory of its architecture.
