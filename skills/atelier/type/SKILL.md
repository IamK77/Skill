# type

Explore typographic voices, rhythm, and multilingual expression. Use to generate type directions, not to force a modular scale or impose one cultural model of text.

## Starting point

Typography gives language a body and a pace. Ask who seems to be speaking, how long a thought feels, and what happens when the language changes.

Bring a question, fragment, reference, or tension. Follow whichever association becomes useful; these invitations are not a sequence to complete. Speculation should remain distinguishable from an observed fact.

## Thought experiments

### Cast a different narrator

Imagine the same content voiced by a navigator, an editor, and a laboratory notebook. Change weight, measure, rhythm, or hierarchy to express the difference.

### Translate rhythm across scripts

Start with real text in the scripts the product serves. Let their different textures suggest a shared family resemblance rather than forcing identical metrics.

### Break the scale on purpose

Try one discontinuity: a tiny label beside a large statement, an unexpected pause, or a dense block amid spacious text. What meaning does that contrast create?

## A possible turn

A financial summary could feel like a ledger through aligned numerals and compact rhythm, or like a letter through narrative hierarchy and generous measure. Both can remain legible; the distinction is the relationship offered to the reader.

## Leave the space open

Offer a few genuinely different possibilities, a sketch, an analogy, or a sharper question when useful. Do not turn every prompt into a scored diagnostic, a completion gate, or a demand for an action plan. The human may choose a direction, combine them, or leave the question open.

A selected direction can become tokens in systems. Actual font loading, fallback behaviour, zoom, and long-text cases belong in implementation checks, not creative permission slips.
