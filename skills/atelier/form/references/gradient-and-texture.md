# Constructing a material relationship

## An opening

Borrow a process such as dye spreading, ink absorbing, or light passing through a surface. Translate one relationship into a gradient or texture, then make a second version with no texture at all. The contrast can reveal whether the material carries meaning or merely covers an unresolved composition.

## Elaborate a candidate

Choose what the gradient or texture is meant to express: illumination, depth, accumulated marks, a transition, or a deliberately abstract field. Keep the information-bearing content distinguishable and compare the material with a flat version. Consider scaling, repetition, image weight, and rendering cost when the candidate moves toward implementation.

## A small comparison

For a layered-paper direction, try a restrained edge transition and a sparse texture, then remove each independently. If neither changes the intended reading, the flat candidate may be clearer. Texture strength and gradient stops are contextual design decisions rather than a preset definition of quality.

## Keep the choice open

Use this note when its question is useful, not as a required inspection. A sketch, a contrast, or an unresolved question can be the result. The [parent lens](../SKILL.md) offers other ways into the subject; execution begins only when a direction and task are chosen.
