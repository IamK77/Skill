# From a visual premise to an experiment

## Several starting points are legitimate

A direction may start with a product fact, an image, a material, a type specimen, a cultural association, or an unexpected composition. Meaning and form can develop together; it is unnecessary to manufacture a one-sentence point of view before making a sketch.

Try an outreach tool as correspondence, as a research notebook, and as a control panel. Each makes a different part of the activity prominent. Keep the sketches distinct before deciding whether any should be combined.

## Make one relationship concrete

Once a direction becomes interesting, express one relationship in a small artifact: a specimen with actual text, a palette applied to two surfaces, a spacing study, or a motion trace. Numbers help reproduce that candidate; they do not make it aesthetically correct.

For example, a modest type ratio can help a dense interface while a large jump can create editorial drama. Neither every size nor every optical adjustment must obey one ratio. Tinted neutrals, pure neutrals, sharp corners, and soft corners are all available materials rather than pass/fail conditions.

## Change one dimension and preserve another

Hold the text hierarchy fixed while changing colour temperature. Hold the layout fixed while changing the narrator through typography. Then try a coherent alternative that changes several dimensions together. The first experiment clarifies a contribution; the second asks whether the premise itself is interesting.

## When implementation begins

Record the chosen values as examples or tokens and check the actual usability and accessibility requirements. Contrast measurements, font-loading observations, and interaction tests constrain a shipped candidate; they do not rank the creative premises that produced it. A sketch or an unresolved pair of directions can be the useful outcome of this note.
