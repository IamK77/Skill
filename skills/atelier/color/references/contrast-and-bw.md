# Contrast, endpoints, and useful distinctions

## Explore contrast as attention

Try the same content with a narrow tonal range and one sharp interruption, then with several clearly separated bands. Ask what becomes prominent and what feels continuous. Contrast can organise a visual argument before it is expressed as a palette specification.

Pure black and pure white are not inherently errors. Off-black, tinted white, and softer endpoints are alternatives with different relationships to surrounding colours, text weight, and viewing conditions. Do not treat one endpoint recipe as a universal comfort law.

## Measure the chosen candidate

Identify the actual foreground/background pairs, including interaction states and surfaces that overlap. Evaluate them against the accessibility requirements adopted for the product and the version of the relevant standard. A contrast formula is a measurement, not a legal determination or a complete account of readability.

Different contrast models should not be described as interchangeable acceptance criteria. If using an additional perceptual model during design, record its role and keep the required conformance check explicit rather than silently substituting one score for another.

## More than hue

Meaningful status, selection, and chart distinctions should not rely on colour alone. Explore labels, shape, position, pattern, or a direct annotation. Check the actual content under relevant viewing conditions; a simulated colour-vision view is useful evidence, not a guarantee about every viewer.

## Example

A chart might use saturated colour for an active series and neutral context for the others. Add direct labels or line patterns where identity must remain distinguishable. The creative choice is what should attract attention; the implementation question is whether the required information remains available.
